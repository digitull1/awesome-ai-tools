## Research (before scripting the numbers)

This video's authority rests on one piece of real research, done on camera-ready paper before the shoot.

1. **Decide the rule before you look.** For example: *"The top five results for 'AI-proof jobs' on {date}, from five different publishers, plus the two oldest comparable lists we can find from those same publishers."* Write the rule down, because it goes on screen.
2. **Record every list:** publisher, headline, URL, publication date and every job on it.
3. **Normalise titles** only where they're clearly the same job ("RN" and "registered nurse"), and note each merge.
4. **Count:** total unique jobs, jobs on every list, jobs on only one list.
5. **Keep the spreadsheet.** Link it in the description so anyone can check the count.

**Outcome branches.** The script has two versions of beat 2. Use whichever the count supports. If the lists broadly agree, title B is off the table; use A or C.

## [0:00–0:35] Hook

**PICTURE:** Host at a table with a stack of printed lists, each headed "AI-proof jobs" or "Jobs AI can't replace".

**HOST:** "Every list of AI-proof jobs you've ever read is a guess."

**PICTURE:** The host picks up the orange stamp and brings it down on the top list: **GUESS**.

**HOST:** "I'm not saying that to be clever. I'm going to show you, in about ninety seconds. And then I'll give you something more useful than a list: a way to check your own job, this week, that works whichever way the future goes."

**ON SCREEN:** EVERY LIST IS A GUESS.

> **Retention:** Contrarian Truth (hook archetype 2) plus a physical prop that matches the thumbnail exactly. It promises proof *and* a replacement, so the viewer isn't asked to give up the list for nothing.

## [0:35–2:15] The lists, compared

**HOST:** "Here's what we did. On {date}, we searched 'AI-proof jobs', took the top {N} lists from different publishers, added {N} older lists from {years}, and wrote down every job on every one."

**ON SCREEN:** A method card: the date, the search, the rule. **All lists linked in the description.**

**PICTURE:** The lists fanned out. A highlighter moves across them. An animated tally builds.

**Branch A · the lists disagree**
> **HOST:** "{N} lists. {Total} different jobs. Jobs that appear on every single list: {X}. Jobs that appear on just one: {Y}. That isn't a consensus. It's a guess, published {N} different ways."

**Branch B · the lists broadly agree**
> **HOST:** "They mostly agree: {X} jobs show up on every list. Which sounds reassuring, until you notice that none of them shows its working. {Only say what the research found, for example that they cite each other or the same single report.} Agreement isn't evidence."

**HOST:** *(either branch)* "And here's the bigger problem. Even if one of these lists were exactly right, it would give you nothing to do today. It can't tell you whether you'd be any good at those jobs, or whether you'd want to be."

> **Retention:** A live reveal with numbers the viewer can check. Either branch pays off the hook honestly, and "nothing to do today" pivots to the practical half.

## [2:15–3:30] Why the lists get it wrong

**HOST:** "The lists get something basic wrong. They answer at the level of job titles. But AI doesn't do job titles. It does tasks."

**PICTURE:** A job-title card, MARKETING MANAGER, splits into task cards: *write briefs · summarise campaign results · approve the budget · calm down an angry client · decide what to cut.*

**HOST:** "Take any job and break it into what you actually do in a week. Some of those tasks, a model can already draft in minutes. Some, it can't touch, like being the person who's responsible when the decision goes wrong. Two people with the same job title can have completely different exposure, depending on what their week is made of."

**ON SCREEN:** Evidence Card: *International Labour Organization, generative AI and jobs research: exposure measured task by task.* **[FACT-CHECK: exact report title, year and wording]**

**HOST:** "So the useful question isn't 'is my job safe?' It's 'which parts of my week are becoming cheap to produce?'"

**ON SCREEN:** ~~IS MY JOB SAFE?~~ → WHICH PARTS OF MY WEEK ARE GETTING CHEAP?

> **Retention:** The reframe is the "oh" moment. The exploding job card is a visual payoff, and it sets up the exercises that follow.

## [3:30–4:45] What seems to be holding value

**HOST:** "So what does seem to be holding up? I'll be careful here, because this is a reading of the evidence so far, not a promise. The pattern looks like this."

**ON SCREEN:** Four cards, one at a time:
**BEING THERE** · **BEING ACCOUNTABLE** · **BEING TRUSTED** · **UNTANGLING MESS**

**HOST:** "Being physically there, where something has to be done or fixed. Being accountable for a decision that affects other people. Being trusted: care, teaching, negotiation. And untangling mess, getting a messy situation to the point where a tool can even be pointed at it."

**ON SCREEN:** Evidence Cards for each source that supports these points. **[FACT-CHECK: remove any source that doesn't directly support the ingredient it sits under]**

**HOST:** "Notice that none of those is a job title. They're ingredients. That's more useful, because you can look for those ingredients in work you already find interesting. Now let's find them in your week."

**HOST:** "Quick one: every week on this channel we test a real job, honestly. If that's useful, subscribe. Right. Your week."

> **Retention:** "Ingredients, not titles" is a satisfying payoff, and the hedge ("a reading, not a promise") earns trust. The single subscribe line lands after the second payoff, around 45% of runtime.

## [4:45–6:15] Exercise 1: audit one week

**ON SCREEN:** EXERCISE 1 · AUDIT ONE WEEK

**HOST:** "Grab last week: your calendar, your to-do list, or just your memory. Write down the main blocks of work. Then mark each one. C for cheap to produce, meaning a model could make a decent first draft. H for hard to replace. No conclusions yet. Just the map."

**ON SCREEN:** Pause card with a 15-second timer.

**PICTURE:** The host's own week on paper, with C and H marked in orange.

**HOST:** "Here's mine. {Real blocks.} {N} Cs, {N} Hs. And the one that surprised me was {real}." **[HOST: real]**

> **Retention:** Participation. The viewer's own week is now in the video. The host's surprise models the honest reaction we want from them.

---

## [6:15–7:45] Exercise 2: the 80% test

**ON SCREEN:** EXERCISE 2 · THE 80% TEST

**HOST:** "Now take the single most repetitive task in your week. Have a model do the first eighty per cent of it. Then judge it like an editor, not a fan."

**PICTURE:** Screen recording of the host's real repetitive task (for example, the weekly update email) given to an AI assistant. Lower third: **assistant name, model or version, and date.** The output, then the host marking it up.

**HOST:** "Here's what I had to fix: {real list}." **[HOST: real]**

**ON SCREEN:** The fix list, highlighted in orange.

**HOST:** "That list is a fairly good description of where your value sits right now. The judgement. The context. Knowing it was confidently wrong."

> **Retention:** A live demo with a result the host couldn't have predicted. It's the most concrete thing most viewers will have heard about AI and their own job.

---

## [7:45–8:30] Exercise 3: the paragraph

**ON SCREEN:** EXERCISE 3 · THE PARAGRAPH

**HOST:** "Last one. It takes twenty minutes, so do it later. Take the part of your work you'd least like a model to do for you, and write down why. Not why it *can't*. Why you wouldn't *want* it to."

**HOST:** "That paragraph is a better compass than any AI-proof list, because it's about you, not the average worker in a report."

> **Retention:** An emotional turn after two analytical exercises. The contrast keeps the energy from flattening in the last third.

## [9:15–9:40] The honest close

**HOST:** "Nobody in this conversation, including us, knows how the next ten years land. Anyone who says they do is guessing. That's not a reason to panic. It's a reason to build a habit instead of placing a bet."
